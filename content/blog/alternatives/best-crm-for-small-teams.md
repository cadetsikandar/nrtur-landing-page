Every small sales team hits the same wall. You've outgrown the spreadsheet, deals are slipping through the cracks, and you decide it's finally time for a real CRM. Then you start shopping — and the market splits into two bad options.

On one side: enterprise platforms that need a multi-week rollout and someone to administer them. On the other: lightweight address books that make you bolt on three other tools just to send a follow-up. Neither was built for a team of two to twenty people that needs to move fast.

This is an honest look at three of the tools small teams most often land on — HubSpot, GoHighLevel, and Less Annoying CRM — where each one breaks down for a small team, and the criteria that actually matter when you choose. Every price here comes from each vendor's own current pricing, checked August 2026; where a number couldn't be verified against a primary source, it's left out rather than guessed.

CRMs cluster at the two ends of a spectrum. Enterprise-grade tools optimize for large organizations with dedicated operations teams — enormous configurability, with the setup burden and price to match. Bare-bones tools optimize for individuals — trivial to start, but they run out of road the moment you need real automation or outreach.

A small sales team lives in the middle and gets pulled toward whichever end it picks: over-buy and drown in setup, or under-buy and duct-tape a stack together. The three tools below are the ones small teams most often reach for, and each is a different version of that same squeeze.

## HubSpot: the freemium cliff

HubSpot is the default recommendation, and for good reasons — the free tier is genuinely capable and the product is polished. The problem is what happens when you grow into it.

The moment you need standard sales automation — sequences, more than a couple of workflows, real reporting — you're pushed from Starter up to Professional. And Professional is a different universe of pricing. Per HubSpot's own pricing, Sales Hub Professional runs **$90/seat/month** (annual), and Marketing Hub Professional is **$800/month** flat for three seats. Professional also carries a **mandatory one-time onboarding fee** — $1,500 for Sales Hub, $3,000 for Marketing Hub.

Two more things catch small teams off guard. Marketing Hub's bill **scales automatically with your marketing-contact count** — cross a tier threshold and the monthly price steps up whether or not you're actively selling to those contacts. And the move from Starter to Professional isn't a gentle upgrade; it's the point where a cheap tool quietly becomes a four- or five-figure annual commitment.

None of this makes HubSpot a bad product. It makes it a product whose economics are built for companies that have already scaled — not for the team still trying to get there.

## GoHighLevel: powerful, but shaped for agencies

GoHighLevel (GHL) markets itself as the all-in-one that replaces your entire stack, and the entry price looks unbeatable: **$97/month** for the Agency Starter plan, per GoHighLevel's pricing. Two caveats matter for a small in-house sales team.

First, that plan is an **agency** construct — it's built around managing up to three client "sub-accounts," not around being one company's sales CRM. You're adopting a tool designed for a different job than the one you have.

Second, the flat fee doesn't include the infrastructure to actually run outreach. Messaging, phone numbers, and AI features are billed **on usage, on top of the subscription** — GHL's "AI Employee" runs **$97/month per sub-account**, and SMS, phone numbers, and email are charged per use. In fairness to GHL, and contrary to a common myth: the A2P 10DLC registration fees it passes on (about $24.49 to register) are **at-cost passthrough** from the carrier registry and Twilio, not a GHL markup. But the base subscription is still just the floor, and your real monthly cost depends heavily on volume.

For a small team that will use a fraction of the platform, GHL often means paying in complexity — a long setup and a wide surface area — for capabilities you won't touch.

## Less Annoying CRM: the simplicity ceiling

At the opposite end sits Less Annoying CRM (LACRM), and it is exactly what its name promises: a clean, flat **$15/user/month**, no tiers, no contracts. For a team that needs shared contacts and a simple pipeline, it's a fair, honest tool.

The ceiling appears when you need to *sell*, not just *store*. Per LACRM's own documentation, it has **no native email sending** — you can log email or use a BCC/forwarding address, but composing and sending happens in your external mail client — and **no built-in multi-step email or SMS sequences**. Outreach automation lives in other tools.

So the low price comes with a hidden "stack tax": you save on the CRM, then pay for a separate email tool, a separate sequencing tool, and the glue (usually Zapier) to connect them. For some teams that's a fine trade. For a team whose core job is outbound follow-up, the missing engine is the whole point.

## What to look for in a small-team CRM

Strip away the brand names and the real question is fit. A CRM that works for a two-to-twenty-person sales team tends to get five things right:

1. **Time-to-value in hours, not weeks.** If basic pipeline and follow-up need a consultant or a multi-week rollout, it's built for a bigger org than yours.
2. **Outreach included, not bolted on.** Native email and multi-step sequences matter — the moment those live in another tool, you're paying a stack tax and stitching data across apps.
3. **Pricing that scales in straight lines.** Per-seat, predictable, with no surprise cliff when you switch on a standard feature or add contacts. Read how the *next* tier is priced before you commit to the first.
4. **Usage costs that are transparent.** If it charges for SMS or calling, the rate should be visible and reasonable — passthrough or a clearly stated margin, not a mystery line item.
5. **A data model that separates who someone is from where they are.** Leads, contacts, companies, and deals as distinct objects — the difference between a pipeline you trust and one full of noise. (We dug into exactly this in [the four-object CRM data model](/blog/guides/leads-vs-contacts-vs-deals/).)

Those criteria are the reason we built **nrtur** the way we did: a focused sales CRM for teams of 1–20+, with native automation and sequences included, flat per-seat pricing (Solo $9, Starter $29, Pro $59, Business $99 — Solo billed flat, the team tiers per seat), and metered SMS billed transparently rather than buried. It won't replace an enterprise deployment or a full agency platform — that's the point. If your team is stuck in the squeeze between too-much and too-little, that's who it's for.

## FAQ

**What's the best CRM for a small sales team?**
There's no single universal answer — it depends on whether your bottleneck is outreach, reporting, or simplicity. Use the five criteria above: fast setup, native outreach, straight-line pricing, transparent usage costs, and a real Lead/Contact/Company/Deal data model. Match those to your actual workflow instead of to a brand's marketing.

**Is HubSpot too expensive for a small business?**
The free and Starter tiers are affordable; the jump to Professional is what stings — $90/seat/month for Sales Hub or $800/month for Marketing Hub, plus a mandatory onboarding fee of $1,500–$3,000, per HubSpot's pricing. The question isn't the entry price, it's whether you can absorb the Professional cliff when you need standard automation.

**Is GoHighLevel good for a small team that isn't an agency?**
It can work, but its entry plan is built around managing client sub-accounts, and messaging and AI features are billed on usage on top of the $97/month base. A small in-house team often pays for breadth it won't use. If you're not running client accounts, weigh that complexity carefully.

**Why can't I send email directly from Less Annoying CRM?**
By design. Per LACRM's documentation, it logs email and uses a BCC/forwarding address rather than sending natively, and it has no built-in sequences. It's a deliberate simplicity choice; the workaround is connecting an external email or marketing tool.

**How do I avoid the CRM "stack tax"?**
Count the tools you'd need alongside the CRM — email, sequencing, automation, and the connectors between them — and price the whole stack, not just the CRM line item. A slightly pricier CRM that includes outreach is often cheaper end-to-end than a cheap CRM plus three add-ons.
