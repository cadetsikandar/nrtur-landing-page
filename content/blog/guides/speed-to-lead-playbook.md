Search "how fast should you follow up with a lead" and you'll get the same line on every page: respond within five minutes and you're **21× more likely to close**. It's repeated so often it reads like physics. It's also almost never sourced — and when you trace it, the real story is more specific, and more useful, than the meme.

Speed to lead is simply the time between someone raising their hand — a demo request, a form fill, a reply — and you getting back to them. Here's what the actual research says, and how to hit that response time without sounding like an autoresponder.

## The research, correctly sourced

Two studies sit behind almost every speed-to-lead stat you've seen. Most articles blur them together. They measured different things.

**The 2007 study — the [Lead Response Management study](https://content.marketingsherpa.com/heap/DG07SFSlides/LeadResponseManagementReport.pdf) by James Oldroyd (InsideSales.com).** Three years of data, six companies, more than 15,000 leads and 100,000 call attempts. Its headline finding is a *comparison between two response times*: calling a lead within **5 minutes instead of 30** made you about **21× more likely to qualify** them — and roughly **100× more likely to even reach** them. Two caveats the internet drops: it's "5 versus 30 minutes" (not "versus a day"), and it measured *contact and qualification*, not closed deals. It's also vendor-published, not a peer-reviewed MIT paper — don't let anyone tell you it is.

**The 2011 study — ["The Short Life of Online Sales Leads,"](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads) Harvard Business Review** (Oldroyd, McElheran & Elkington). An audit of **2,241 U.S. companies**. Firms that responded within an hour were **7× more likely to have a meaningful conversation with a decision-maker** than those that waited just one hour longer — and **60× more likely** than those that waited 24 hours or more. This is the more rigorous and more citable of the two.

Both are over a decade old, from before Slack, mobile push, and AI SDRs. So treat the exact multipliers as directional, not gospel. But the direction has never reversed: the faster you respond, the better you do.

## Why the "2026 benchmarks" deserve skepticism

Here's the part most speed-to-lead posts won't tell you. The shiny recent numbers — "the average company takes 47 hours to respond," "only 23% respond within five minutes," tidy close-rate tables by response time — mostly trace back to a **single vendor's self-reported benchmark**, with no published methodology, quietly republished across dozens of blogs until it reads like consensus. We're not going to launder those into facts.

The one recent study with real method behind it is worth more than all of them combined: a "secret shopper" audit by Drift submitted forms to **433 companies** and found only **7% responded within five minutes**, while **55% hadn't responded within five business days**. That's the honest takeaway — not a precise close-rate table, but a simple, well-earned one: **most companies are still slow, which means speed is still an edge sitting there for anyone willing to take it.**

## How to actually respond in five minutes

You can't hit a five-minute response by asking reps to watch their inbox. It has to be built into the system.

1. **Capture instantly.** A form submit writes a [lead](/blog/guides/leads-vs-contacts-vs-deals/) into your CRM in real time — no overnight CSV imports, no manual entry.
2. **Acknowledge automatically.** An instant auto-reply confirms you got the message and sets the expectation that a human is coming. (Do this well — see below.)
3. **Route and notify.** The lead is auto-assigned to a rep by territory or round-robin, and that rep gets a *push notification*, not an email they'll see after lunch.
4. **Hand off to a human, fast.** A real person picks up within minutes for the actual conversation.

## The one rule: automated speed shouldn't sound automated

A five-minute response that reads like a form letter barely beats a slow one. The goal isn't just fast — it's fast *and* specific enough that the person believes a human actually looked.

**Weak — generic and obviously canned:**
> "Dear Valued Customer, thank you for your interest in our enterprise solutions. Please find attached our 30-page brochure…"

**Strong — specific and human:**
> "Hi Sam — saw you just asked for a tour of nrtur. I'm pulling up your account now. Do you have five minutes today, or should I grab a time on your calendar here [link]?"

The difference isn't tone, it's evidence. The second message shows someone registered *what you actually did*. That's what keeps a fast response from feeling like spam.

## Checklist: pressure-test your setup

- [ ] Form submissions land in your CRM in real time, with zero manual entry.
- [ ] There's a written response-time SLA, enforced by the system — not by reminding reps to check email.
- [ ] New leads are auto-assigned by territory or round-robin, instantly.
- [ ] Reps get a push notification, not just an email.
- [ ] An automated acknowledgment goes out before a human can, so the lead knows they were heard.
- [ ] You track response time as a metric — not just deals closed — so you can see when you're slipping.

## FAQ

**Is the "21× more likely" stat still true today?**
It's from a 2007 study about phone calls, and it specifically compares a 5-minute response to a 30-minute one. The exact multiplier may differ now, but every study since points the same direction, and the 2011 HBR data backs it up. Treat it as directional, not a guarantee.

**What's a realistic response-time target for a small team?**
Five minutes during business hours is the goal, but the biggest, cheapest win is simply beating the one-hour mark — that's where the HBR data shows the sharpest drop-off. Under an hour beats "sometime today" by a wide margin.

**Can automation replace the rep?**
No — only the first touch. Use automation to acknowledge instantly and handle light routing, then get a human into the conversation as fast as possible. An auto-reply that pretends to be a person, or tries to close, backfires.

**We can't staff this 24/7. What's the minimum?**
An instant automated acknowledgment, then a human follow-up the same business day. The single worst outcome is silence for hours while the lead talks to whoever answered first.
